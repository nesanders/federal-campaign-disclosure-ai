AI Use in Federal Campaign Disclosures

A read of federal campaign-finance disclosures for U.S. House and Senate candidates, looking for payments to AI vendors and how that spending breaks down by vendor, stated purpose, party, incumbency, candidate age, and chamber — and how each of those has changed across recent election cycles.

This extends the Washington Post's September 2026 reporting on OpenAI/ChatGPT spending in campaign filings to a broader set of AI vendors and a fuller set of cross-cuts, using the same public FEC disclosure data.

Overview

1. Which AI vendors are campaigns paying?

The Post's analysis looked only at OpenAI. Here every disbursement's payee name, stated purpose, category description, and memo text is matched against a taxonomy of both general-purpose AI vendors (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, xAI, Perplexity, and AI-native creative tools) and vendors built specifically for campaigns. The taxonomy started from vendors named in press coverage (Quiller, BattlegroundAI, Chorus AI, DonorAtlas, RivalMind, Aaru), then was expanded by scanning the disclosures themselves for AI-indicative language and researching the payee names that kept turning up. That second pass is what found the biggest vendors on this page by dollar volume — AI-driven texting and call-time platforms like Amplify.ai, CallTime.AI, Prompt.io, and Numero — which is why the ranking below looks different from what most coverage of "AI in campaigns" would lead you to expect. See methodology for the full list, its confidence tiers, and the vendors we considered and rejected.

The same candidate-committee, high-confidence disbursements, tracked across the 2020, 2022, 2024, and 2026 two-year cycles. 5. Who's spending the most — in dollars, and as a share of their budget?

Every candidate committee or PAC/party committee with at least one high-confidence AI-vendor disbursement, across all cycles. "% of total spend" divides AI spend by that committee's total reported operating expenditure that cycle (memo entries excluded to avoid double-counting — see methodology); the share-of-budget table is limited to committees spending at least $5,000 in total that cycle, so a $50 total-spend committee with one $40 AI purchase doesn't read as an 80% AI budget. Methodology & data provenance

This site is generated entirely from public federal disclosure data by an automated pipeline; there is no manual curation of the numbers themselves beyond the vendor/category taxonomy below.
